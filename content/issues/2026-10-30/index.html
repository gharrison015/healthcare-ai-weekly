UnitedHealthcare's AI Agent Hits 21 Million Members
The largest commercial insurer in the country just put an AI agent in front of 21 million members. Whatever it does, it sets the floor for what every other payer will be asked to do by their boards next quarter.
UnitedHealthcare says its initial rollout showed 'significant success' before expanding to 21 million members. That phrasing should make you cautious. Significant success on what metric? Call deflection? Member satisfaction scores? Prior auth throughput? If the metric isn't tied to a clinical or financial outcome your clients can replicate, it's vapor metrics dressed in a press release. Here's the second-order thing to watch: AI agents make member interaction cheap. Which means UHC can handle 10x the volume of benefits inquiries without adding headcount. That sounds like efficiency. It is also the architecture for making it harder, not easier, for members to reach a human when the AI gets it wrong. The authority map problem is real here. A September 2026 survey found 28% of healthcare organizations had agentic AI in production. UHC just moved that number in a very visible way. For your payer clients, the question isn't 'should we build an AI agent' anymore. The question is 'what are the guardrails, and who owns the liability when the agent denies something it shouldn't?' That question doesn't have a clean answer yet. The AMA published guidance this week on AI liability, but it's a framework, not a binding standard. Your clients need to be asking vendors right now: what happens when your agent makes a decision that harms a patient? Who is on the hook? Don't accept a terms-of-service answer. Get it in the contract.
Risk angle: UnitedHealthcare's trust deficit with patients and providers is enormous right now. An AI agent that handles prior auth, benefits navigation, or claims appeals becomes a liability multiplier if it makes one high-profile wrong call. The PR exposure here is real, not theoretical.

Epic's AI Bill: '$10 or $10K.' Nobody Knows.
Health system CFOs are about to get hit with AI costs from Epic they can't model, can't cap, and can't easily opt out of. This is the conversation your clients need to have before they sign anything.
Epic's AI pricing model is consumption-based, and nobody at the health system level has a clean answer for what their actual spend will look like at scale. The $10 to $10,000 range cited this week isn't a bug in their pricing transparency. It reflects the genuinely variable nature of how AI features get used across different Epic modules, different specialties, and different patient volumes. That's not a reason to avoid it. It's a reason to get the contract right before you deploy. Here's the thing your clients need to understand: Epic is not a vendor you negotiate with like a typical SaaS contract. Their market share exceeds 37% of U.S. hospital beds. That leverage runs one direction. But there are still moves available. First, push for consumption caps with escalation clauses tied to documented ROI. Second, require audit rights on AI usage billing, because right now most systems can't verify what they're being charged for. Third, separate the AI modules you actually need from the ones Epic is packaging in. 'Included in your Enterprise Agreement' often means 'you're paying for it whether you use it or not.' Meanwhile, Larry Ellison is openly pitching Oracle AI to Epic hospitals this week. The argument is 'maybe ours is better.' That's not a vendor pitch. That's a wedge strategy. Oracle's health AI play through the Cerner install base is real, and they are actively trying to flip Epic customers. Your clients should know this leverage exists even if they have no intention of switching. And STAT ran a piece this week arguing Epic's dominance is actively choking health tech innovation. That narrative is building, and it will eventually reach regulators.
Risk angle: Epic's pricing unpredictability is a feature from Epic's perspective and a serious budgeting problem from the health system's perspective. When the range is $10 to $10,000, you don't have a budget item. You have an open tab.

Doximity Drops Proof That General AI Gets Drug Doses Dangerously Wrong
Physicians are already using ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini to look up dosing information. Doximity just showed that these tools give dangerously wrong answers at a rate that matters clinically. This is the liability briefing your CMO clients need now.
Doximity's research is self-serving in the sense that their dose verification tool is the product being sold. But the research finding stands independent of that: general-use AI models including the major frontier models get medication doses wrong, and some of those errors are in the dangerous range. Not rounding errors. Not outdated formulary information. Errors that could harm a patient who received the wrong dose. Here is the clinical governance problem this creates. Physicians and pharmacists are already using ChatGPT and similar tools to cross-check dosing, especially for off-label indications, pediatric weights, and renal dose adjustments where the clinical literature is scattered. These are exactly the use cases where the models perform worst because the training data is thinner and more variable. The Doximity finding is a direct input to the AMA liability framework published this same week. The AMA said health systems, not just vendors, carry liability when AI harms a patient. If your health system client has not explicitly blocked clinical AI use outside of approved tools, they have an undisclosed liability sitting in physician workflows right now. Here's my challenge for CMOs and CIOs. One: conduct a rapid audit of what AI tools physicians are actually using at the point of care, not just what's on the approved list. Two: get your clinical informatics team to test the major general-purpose models against your formulary for the 20 highest-risk drug categories. Three: if you're running ambient scribing tools that surface medication information to clinicians, verify that the dosing information is sourced from a validated clinical database, not the underlying LLM.
Risk angle: The second-order effect is the one that stings: AI makes clinical information lookup cheap, which means clinicians reach for it more. Which means the rate of dangerous wrong answers goes up in proportion to adoption. Doximity's own tool outperformed general models, which is conveniently timed research, but the underlying finding about general model errors is real.

OpenEvidence is doing something strategically important that most vendors aren't: they're embedding directly in the EHR rather than building a parallel app that clinicians have to context-switch to use. NYU Langone's 12 million annual visits is not a pilot number. That is systemwide production deployment. The clinical AI search category is getting crowded fast. Vera Health raised $28.5M this week for a competing evidence-graded clinical search platform, now at 800,000 physician users globally. The Doximity research this week showed the exact problem both companies are trying to solve: general-purpose AI gives dangerous clinical answers. Purpose-built clinical search with evidence grading and source attribution is the counter. The EHR integration play is the right one. Clinicians don't leave the chart to look things up if they can avoid it. The tool that lives inside Epic wins by default even if a competing tool is technically superior. OpenEvidence's NYC concentration is a deliberate strategy. Land the marquee academic centers first, use that credibility to go upmarket to national IDNs. For your AMC and academic health system clients, the vendor landscape in clinical AI search is moving to a two-horse race between OpenEvidence and Vera Health plus a handful of specialty-specific players. The time to evaluate is now, before Epic builds its own version and the choice is made for them.
Risk angle: OpenEvidence is building concentration risk in New York City's academic medical centers. When one company's clinical AI is the default at NYU Langone, Weill Cornell, and potentially others in the same market, you get a single point of failure embedded in care delivery across millions of patients.

Value-Based Care Watch
Arbital Health Builds the Actuarial Brain VBC Actually Needs
Value-based care has always had a data problem: the actuarial models that price risk were built for fee-for-service logic and duct-taped into VBC contracts. Arbital is building purpose-built actuarial AI for VBC from the ground up, which is the missing infrastructure layer that makes risk contracts actually work.
Hospitalogy's piece on Arbital Health and the companion piece on missing VBC infrastructure arrived the same week MDX Hawaii deployed Innovaccer's Atlas platform across 6,000 independent clinicians. Read together, they describe the same problem from two angles. The population health management infrastructure exists. The claims analytics infrastructure exists. What doesn't exist in most VBC organizations is the actuarial intelligence layer that connects them: a system that can continuously reprice risk exposure based on real-time clinical signals, not just retrospective claims. Traditional actuarial models are batch processes. They price a population at contract signing and adjust annually. VBC demands continuous repricing because the population is dynamic, the care interventions change the risk profile in real time, and the benchmarks shift. Arbital's claim is that they've built AI that does this continuously. The MDX Hawaii deployment is a parallel data point: they're using Innovaccer's Atlas to unify population health data across 6,000 clinicians in an IPA structure. The operational question is whether Atlas's analytics layer feeds the actuarial intelligence layer or whether those remain two separate systems that don't talk to each other. For your VBC clients, here's the diagnostic question to ask: can your current actuarial model ingest a real-time clinical event from your EHR and update your risk exposure estimate within 24 hours? If the answer is no, you have the problem Arbital is solving.
Risk angle: Actuarial AI that gets risk pricing wrong in a VBC contract doesn't just hurt margins. It can sink a care delivery organization that took on more risk than the model said they could handle. The stakes of a bad actuarial model in VBC are existential, not just operational.

84% of Payer Execs See No AI ROI. The Exception-Handling Bill Is Why.
70% of health plans have deployed AI. 84% of their executives say they haven't seen ROI. That gap is the single most important data point for any health system or payer evaluating their AI portfolio right now.
The Everest Group and Sagility research cited in HIT Consultant this week puts a number on something consultants have been saying informally for two years: health plans are buying AI and not seeing returns. The 84% figure is striking but not surprising. The HIT Consultant piece on exception handling from the same week explains the mechanism. Every AI deployment in claims, prior auth, or coding has an exception rate. When the AI isn't sure, it routes to a human. When the AI is wrong, a human fixes it. When the AI creates a new error type that the human workflow wasn't designed to catch, the organization builds a new exception process. Those exception processes require headcount, supervision, and quality checks. None of that cost appears in the vendor's ROI projection because the vendor's model assumes the automation handles 95% of cases cleanly. In production, it often handles 70% cleanly, 20% with human review, and 10% with human correction. The labor cost for that 30% often exceeds the savings on the 70%. The revenue cycle AI category is particularly exposed to this dynamic. RCM AI is sold as denial reduction and coding accuracy. The exception handling reality is that denials get more sophisticated, not less, because payers use AI to flag more subtle errors. So you automate your coding and your payer automates their denial logic, and you end up with the same denial rate but now two AI systems and the exception handling costs for both. The agentic AI framing in the HIT Consultant revenue cycle piece is the right direction: agents that can take action on exceptions, not just route them to humans. But that requires trust and authority maps that most health systems aren't ready to grant.
Risk angle: The hidden cost nobody is modeling is exception handling: what happens when the AI gets it wrong and a human has to fix it. That remediation cost wipes out the automation savings, and it's not in any vendor ROI calculation you've ever seen.

M&A & Partnerships
Zocdoc Opens Booking Rails to AI Agents
Zocdoc just made its booking infrastructure machine-readable. When AI agents can book appointments autonomously, the access layer between patients and care delivery is no longer controlled by the provider's front desk. That's a structural shift.
Zocdoc CEO Oliver Kharraz called this a 'contrarian move' because the healthcare industry is still wary of agentic AI. He's right that it's contrarian. He's also right that it's directionally correct. Here's the thing about agentic scheduling: it makes appointment booking cheap, which means fake appointment booking also gets cheap. A patient's health AI agent can scan 14 specialists, book appointments with all of them, and cancel 13 after the first available responds. That's the Potemkin village version of access: it looks like the patient is being served, but the provider's schedule is full of phantom slots. The economics flip when AI agents are both booking and canceling at scale. No-show rates, which are already a significant revenue problem for specialty practices, could spike in ways that aren't attributable to patient behavior but to agent behavior on the patient's behalf. The more important long-term signal is what this does to the relationship between providers and referral sources. If AI agents are booking directly on behalf of patients based on availability and insurance match rather than physician referral, the traditional referral network starts to erode. PCPs lose the routing control they currently have. Health systems with captive referral networks need to think about what happens to that network when the booking layer is open to any AI agent with API access.

FDA and CMS Are Racing to Prevent a Federal-State AI Regulation Collision

STAT reported this week that FDA and CMS are in active behind-the-scenes discussions to head off a conflict between state-level AI regulatory sandboxes and federal oversight requirements. Several states have moved forward with programs that let health systems test clinical AI under modified liability and oversight rules. The problem is those sandbox rules in some cases conflict with FDA's Software as a Medical Device framework and CMS's conditions of participation for Medicare and Medicaid. This is not a theoretical regulatory dispute. It's a practical problem for health systems operating in states with sandbox programs who are trying to run clinical AI pilots. If the state says 'you have regulatory cover to test this,' and FDA says 'that AI output constitutes a diagnostic claim and requires 510(k) clearance,' the health system is caught in the middle. The immediate guidance for clients in sandbox states: do not assume state-level regulatory cover extends to federal requirements. Get separate legal and regulatory review for any clinical AI pilot operating under a state sandbox program. The longer-term watch: if FDA and CMS succeed in creating a federal-state harmonization framework for clinical AI, it could actually accelerate legitimate AI deployment by creating a clear pathway. If they don't, you get a patchwork that makes innovation slower and more expensive for health systems trying to operate across state lines.
